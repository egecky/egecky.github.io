What Low-Rank EGGROLL Actually Does

September 27, 2026

Its mean update, its variance, and a one-evaluation alternative

Evolution strategies train models by sampling parameter changes, evaluating them, and using the scores to choose an update. EGGROLL makes this search cheaper by replacing a dense random matrix with a product of two thin Gaussian factors. Every sampled update is low rank, yet its covariance is the identity, giving nonzero variance along every matrix direction. That combination raises a useful question: does EGGROLL’s average update behave like dense Gaussian evolution strategies, and what does low rank cost when we estimate that update with a finite population?

The paper answers these questions separately. It derives the exact mean field and shows how it can depart from the gradient of the smoothed objective, then quantifies the extra sampling variance due to finite rank. Finally, it uses the same population more efficiently: LOO-ROLL replaces EGGROLL’s antithetic pair with one fitness evaluation per direction and a leave-one-out baseline.

Figure 1. Finite-rank product perturbations distort the frequency geometry: the mean-field direction can tilt away from the smoothed-gradient direction. Smaller radius and higher rank move the transformation toward the dense-Gaussian case.

Low-rank samples change the mean update

Identity covariance is not the whole story. It describes averages over perturbations, not the geometry of each draw: a rank-one matrix cannot itself point in most full-rank directions. EGGROLL also evaluates these product perturbations with the score of a dense Gaussian. Those two ingredients combine to produce a mean field that is not generally the gradient of the smoothed objective.

In Fourier coordinates, each matrix mode is transformed by an inverse operator, (I + σ²L/r)⁻¹, applied to the smoothed gradient. This is a resolvent filter: it attenuates different matrix directions by different amounts rather than simply shrinking the whole gradient by one scalar. At finite rank, that directional change can even make the mean field nonconservative, so there need not be any scalar objective whose gradient equals the update.

There is an important boundary case. For every quadratic objective, antithetic EGGROLL recovers the exact gradient in expectation at every rank and perturbation radius. The discrepancy comes from higher-order variation of the objective across the perturbations, not from arbitrary linear terms or coupled quadratic curvature. At finite radius, that nonlinear variation can matter for stability.

Figure 2. A strict local maximum of the objective and its smoothed version can become repelling under the rank-one mean field. In this example, rank two and dense Gaussian ES remain locally attracting.

The example makes the distinction concrete: the objective’s origin is a strict local maximum, but the rank-one mean field has a positive eigenvalue of about 0.029, so nearby points move away along one direction. Rank two is locally attracting in the same construction. The lesson is not that rank one always fails; it is that identity covariance alone cannot guarantee the same finite-radius dynamics as dense Gaussian ES.

The finite-rank variance cost is small for wide matrices

Mean-field bias and sampling variance are different costs. In a local affine model, the total variance of a rank-r estimate has the dense-Gaussian factor mn + 1 plus a finite-rank surcharge 2(m+n+1)/r. For a square weight matrix of width w, the surcharge relative to dense Gaussian ES is 2(2w+1)/[r(w²+1)], which scales as 1/(rw). At width 4096, the rank-one increase is about 0.098%. Thus low rank can noticeably change the expected direction without adding much sampling noise for a wide matrix.

LOO-ROLL spends each evaluation once

The usual antithetic estimator evaluates both +σE and −σE for every sampled direction. LOO-ROLL instead evaluates one side for each member of the population, then subtracts the average fitness of the other members as that sample’s baseline. Because this baseline is independent of the held-out member’s perturbation, the resulting estimator is unbiased for the raw EGGROLL mean field before same-population score standardization. It keeps the low-rank factors and the rest of EGGROLL’s efficient machinery; it changes how the population’s fitness values are used.

On transformer blocks, the leave-one-out estimator cuts update mean-squared error by roughly 49–50% at equal evaluation cost across the tested population sizes. At the same number of directions, its error is at most 5.2% higher, falling to 0.8% at a population of 128. A separate audit finds that at this population size the raw and standardized rank-one mean updates have cosine similarity 0.99970.

At equal time, fewer evaluations buy more training

The practical test is whether the saved evaluation can improve a complete post-training run, not just a single update. The paper compares LOO-ROLL and antithetic EGGROLL at matched wall time over 14 model–task settings, using five paired seeds per setting. The suite includes Qwen3 models from 0.6B to 14B parameters and SmolLM2-1.7B. Eleven of the 14 settings improve significantly in individual paired tests, and nine remain significant after Holm correction; none significantly favors EGGROLL. Together, the matched-time results make LOO-ROLL the paper’s clearest practical contribution: it preserves EGGROLL’s low-rank implementation while using the available evaluation budget more effectively.

What the results establish

The theory explains why low-rank EGGROLL can be both statistically economical and dynamically different from dense Gaussian ES. Its extra affine-model variance shrinks with matrix width, but its finite-radius mean field can still tilt because the product perturbation law is not rotationally symmetric. LOO-ROLL addresses a separate bottleneck by turning the antithetic evaluation budget into a larger one-sided population with a leave-one-out baseline.

The exact mean-field statements concern the raw estimator before same-population score standardization, and the variance formula is exact in the affine model. The post-training evidence spans two model families and the tested tasks and scales through 14B; it does not settle behavior at larger scales or under every training recipe. A natural next step is to study how population size and score processing interact across broader post-training settings.
